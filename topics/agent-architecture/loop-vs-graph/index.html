Loop Engineering vs Graph Engineering
— two AI agent buzzwords, taken slowly

Channel Sean's AI Stories (Shen Sean Chen) · 21 min 31 sec · published 2 August 2026 · open the video

The whole video in three sentences

1. Loops and graphs are not competitors — they do two different jobs, and any system worth building uses both.

2. Reach for a loop when you know the goal but not the steps; reach for a graph when you already know the shape of the work and want those steps to move together.

3. Yes, a graph looks like a 2023 deterministic workflow (Airflow, Step Functions) — but three things are genuinely new: a node can be an LLM call, the model itself can pick which edge to take, and the flow now needs guards.

01Why is everyone suddenly arguing about this?

0:00 – 0:55

Sean opens not with the technicals but with where the noise came from. On 18 July 2026, Peter Steinberger — the author of "OpenClaw", the project with the little lobster — threw one question onto X:

"are we still talking about loops, or did we shift to graphs yet?" — Peter Steinberger on X, 18 July 2026 · roughly 3 million views

The top reply was just someone joking that they were on vacation and would like the discourse to stop. Two weeks later the entire AI agent space was arguing over whether loop engineering was dead.

Sean's point here is worth holding onto: a term usually goes viral not because it suddenly became important, but because someone with reach said it out loud. Exactly what happened to "context engineering". So don't panic every time a new buzzword lands — go find out what actually changed first.

02The AI agent engineering ladder (three years in one chart)

2:00 – 8:22 · the most valuable stretch of the video

Before comparing loops and graphs, Sean draws a ladder: five rungs we've climbed since 2023. Each rung exists because the one below it wasn't enough. Once you see the order, every buzzword falls into place.

1Prompt Engineering — give it a role The ChatGPT 3.5 era. Sean's most accurate word for it: role playing. "You are the best poet, like Shakespeare — write me a poem about this view in Switzerland." You're steering how the model talks, and nothing else. It has no material to work from yet.
2Context Engineering — give it data The moment LLMs moved from a consumer toy into real workflows, prompts stopped being enough. A customer-service bot or a sales agent has to be fed actual data: customer history sitting in Excel, Google Sheets, a CRM. The focus shifts from "how it speaks" to "what information makes it into the context window".
3Skills — give it an SOP Teaching a procedure. In agent-harness language this is procedural memory. Sean's analogy: telling a kid "when you walk home, walk on the right-hand side if you're in the US or China, the left if you're in the UK or Japan." No extra data needed — just a rule that fires when a situation comes up. Why bother? So you stop repeating yourself forever ("don't use emoji, don't use emoji…").
4Loop — give it a goal and a toolbox Sometimes you have a goal but no idea of the exact steps. So you tell the model: "here's your goal — fix the bug this customer reported; here's a pile of tools: web search, the GitHub API, Gmail APIs, Google Calendar, MCP servers. Use them. Loop until the goal is done." This is where Anthropic's MCP starts paying off.
5Graph — give it a route map A procedure that is decided in advance. Plenty of people push back that this isn't new — since 2023 we've had Airflow and Step Functions for deterministic workflows, precisely so that not every decision gets handed to the model. Because for a lot of tasks we do know the steps; there's already an SOP.

What Sean concludes from the ladder

The arc isn't "the new thing replaces the old thing" — it's a pendulum that swings out and settles in the middle:

Skills = follow a strict procedure → Loop = "agent, go figure it out yourself" → Graph = a mixture of the two.

His practical order: write the skill first. When you notice the same pattern repeating across your workflows — especially in a company, say an e-commerce support desk answering logistics, refund, and sample-check questions the same way every time — consolidate it into a graph. Then inside that graph, keep a loop wherever the work is genuinely exploratory. Deep research, he notes, was one of the earliest examples of loop engineering: "go search the internet, I want a report."

03What a LOOP actually looks like

8:22 – 9:23

Sean's framing: a loop discovers what to do next. The model decides, one step at a time.

Analogy: a loop is taking a taxi. You give the destination and nothing else. The driver decides every turn, whether to double back when traffic hits, and how long it takes. You don't know the route when you get in.

04What a GRAPH actually looks like

9:23 – 10:58

A graph is for when you already know the shape. Sean's example is his morning routine — "who opened pull requests overnight, what's the latest AI agent news, what's on my calendar today". Because you know exactly what needs checking, all of it can run at the same time instead of queueing.

Analogy: a graph is a metro line. The track is already laid, the stations are fixed, and several trains can be moving on different lines at once. You know the shape of the journey before you board.

05Loop vs graph — where the difference actually is

LoopGraph
Who picks the next stepThe model, one step at a timeYou, up front — the shape is drawn
What you hand itA goal plus a set of toolsA map of nodes and edges
How it runsSequentially, one after anotherIn parallel, many branches at once
Best forExploratory work with no SOP: debugging, deep research, "go find out for me"Repeat work whose pattern has settled: morning briefs, ticket triage, support flows
WeaknessSlow and hard to predict — it can spin longer than you'd likeRigid — if the workflow is still changing, a graph gets in the way
Use it whenYou know the goal, not the routeYou know the shape, and want it all to move together

Sean's answer to "which one is better": neither. A graph doesn't replace a loop, because the two coexist and answer different needs.

06The difference you actually feel: time

15:36 – 17:00

This is the part that makes it click. Sean lays the two approaches on a timeline for the same task. The loop calls tools one at a time because every step waits on the model's next decision. The graph was told up front "you need these four tools", so all four set off together.

07Into the code: Waku Agent

10:58 – 19:00

Whiteboard done, Sean opens a terminal. Waku Agent is his open source project — a local-first AI assistant, sitting at 700+ GitHub stars. Setup is pip install waku-agent, fill in your environment keys, then launch the dashboard on localhost (he's on port 7777 in the demo).

The full harness

The two graphs he demos live

GraphHow it's invokedWhat happens
triage what's up today Two branches fire together: (a) classify whether this needs a serious agent call, and (b) go straight to Google Calendar. It then drops into a loop to call list events / read Apple Calendar before answering.
gather /gather tell me what's up with waku agent… Four tools run simultaneously: GitHub, web search, calendar, memory. The results are synthesised into a single morning brief — PRs waiting for review, a package that just shipped, plus web research findings (in the demo: Harrison Chase's writing on harnesses & memory, and harness eval rankings).

Note what this proves: both graphs still use loops inside them — the "web search" node has to keep searching until it finds something, and the "check calendar" node has to keep digging until it knows what's there. Concrete evidence that the two concepts stack rather than compete.

What's inside the folder

  • waku/graph/ — holds the graph engine: you add nodes and add edges. That's essentially all of it.
  • A node can be anything: a tool call, an LLM call, an agent call, an MCP server, or a router.
  • An edge answers one question: once this node finishes, where next? (After the web search, go straight to synthesis, or stop somewhere else first?)
  • waku/graph/workflows/ — one .py file per workflow: triage.py, gather.py. The contents are simple: define the nodes, define the edges, done.

Sean closes the section with an invitation: drop your own .py file into workflows, submit it as a contribution, and once it's approved your workflow shows up when anyone types /graphs.

08The closing question: "isn't this just a 2023 DAG?"

20:26 – end

Sean deliberately saves the sharpest criticism for last, then answers it. Structurally, yes — this is the same directed acyclic graph we've had for years. But he argues three things are genuinely new:

What's newWhat it means
The nodes are non-deterministicIn an old DAG, a node was a function that returned the same thing every time. Now a node can be an LLM call — the output can differ on every run.
The model can pick the edgeRouting gets handed to the model as a judge (LLM-as-a-judge): "is this simple enough for a cheap model, or does it need the stronger one?" An old DAG could never do that.
It needs guardsBecause the nodes and the routing are uncertain, the flow needs guardrails that a deterministic pipeline never required.

And the closing line I found the most honest: these are buzzwords — but buzzwords go viral for one of two reasons: a famous person said it, or it's actually useful. Telling the two apart is the job.

09What to take away

  • Don't pick a side. The question isn't "loop or graph" — it's "which parts of my workflow have a settled pattern, and which parts still need exploring".
  • Start with the cheapest rung. Prompt → context → skill. Only climb to a loop when the steps genuinely can't be predicted, and only to a graph once you see the same pattern coming round again and again.
  • Don't rush into a graph while the workflow is still shifting. Graphs pay off once the process is stable; before that you're just relocating the complexity.
  • A graph buys you speed and predictability, not intelligence. The intelligence still comes from the loops living inside the nodes.
  • For banking and enterprise contexts — heavy on SOPs, heavy on audit trails — graphs are unusually relevant: the steps are traceable, the routes are auditable, and the uncertain parts stay fenced inside specific nodes.
